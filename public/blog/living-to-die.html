Living to Die

a review of Kazuo Ishiguro's 2005 novel, Never Let Me Go

October 6, 2026 • CW: spoilers

4/5

In a way, Never Let Me Go by Kazuo Ishiguro can be interpreted as a post-apocalyptic story. Like animals raised for slaughter, Never Let Me Go features human clones that are produced and raised in special facilities separate from the rest of humanity. When they are of age, the clones are forced to donate their organs until they die. However, three differences make Never Let Me Go unique from other post-apocalyptic stories. First, the clones alone face a world doomed from the start. The rest of humanity actually benefits from their loss. Secondly, the clones largely learn to accept their fates and don't fight against the system to survive. Finally, the apocalypse is perpetual. An unknown number of clones must die each day to grant humans longer lives. In the story, the narrator, who is also a clone, Kathy, recalls her childhood at a boarding-school-like facility called Hailsham. She and the other clone children experience a relatively typical childhood, growing up around friends, learning, and partaking in creative activities. They hold only a very shallow understanding of their purpose as clones, until they get older and begin to learn more. Eventually, the students are moved to various other facilities, where they live until they begin donations. Kathy and her close friends Ruth and Tommy meet older students, whom they call veterans. The veterans expose them to the world beyond Hailsham, and suggest that Kathy and Tommy, who is also Kathy's love interest, apply for a couples' deferral from donation. Ultimately, they learn that deferrals were made up, and nothing they do can prevent their fates. At the end of the book, all of Kathy's friends have died from donations, and Tommy also passes away under Kathy's care.

The end of Never Let Me Go left a bittersweet taste in my mouth. Ishiguro drags us through an entire childhood, from elementary school to college student-age, exposing us to the full range of emotions felt by children. The clones are as human as anyone else, so we root for the protagonists, praying for their survival and overcoming of the system that raises them to die. Sadly, nothing diverts them from their destiny.

Through the childhood naivety and false hope in their final years, the characters of Never Let Me Go are very thoroughly developed. The book does a good job convincing us of the humanity within the clones by walking us through hope and love, but also conflict and drama. We watch as Tommy struggles with his violent temper. We see Ruth's boastful nature as she tries to stand out among her peers. These characters are full of imperfections; no luck, no plot armor, and no outstanding abilities.

I think Never Let Me Go delivers two messages: One is that our humanity comes not from our birth circumstance, but from the range of emotions we can feel. The clones live short lives, and from this limitation, stay youthful and are keen to produce and hang onto any hope they have of a future. Based on this, our protagonists are just as human, if not more, than their naturally born counterparts. The second message builds off the first, telling us that hope is key to happiness. Fate may not change, but your decision on how to view and use the time you have can affect everything. From the clones' perspective, their life is an apocalypse that they were born into and must accept. Only through acknowledgement can they find peace.

Never Let Me Go is an excellent but heartbreaking novel. (There's also a movie, but I haven't seen it.) It naturally prompts questions about humanity, the ethics of farming, and why life is worth living despite the fact that everyone dies someday. It doesn't answer those questions for you, instead, its nonlinear structure leaves you back at the start of the cruel cycle, only with a new perspective.
