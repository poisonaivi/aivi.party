The Need to Know: How "The Machine Stops" Makes Knowledge Obsolete

September 21, 2026

E.M. Forster's era was demarcated by a period of rapid innovation and scientific achievement. Many such achievements clearly serve as the inspiration of the futuristic technologies included in his 1909 short story, "The Machine Stops," such as airships, pneumatic post, and wireless video communication. Forster uses these fictionalized versions of early 20th century inventions to extrapolate the pernicious effects of overdependence on technology. Ultimately, the story warns us about the fine line between technological progress and self destruction. The same force driving humanity towards an easier, laid-back life can also cause us to regress until we are too weak to stand on our own. The killer is not progress itself, but rather the unsustainable nature of mass overdependence on technology and declining agency led on by humanity's tendency to pick relaxation over effort.

The Industrial Revolution at its Logical Extreme

The first technology mentioned in the story, capable of filling a room "with a soft radiance" (Forster, p. 1) despite the absence of sunlight or lamps, alludes to the growing use of electric indoor lighting in Forster's time period. Invented in the same year as Forster's birth (Matulka), Thomas Edison's incandescent light bulb was revolutionary, replacing unreliable sunlight and flammable oil lamps with a switch on the wall, and opening doors that allowed people to use more of the day and night. The Machine's use of such industrial inventions perfectly encapsulates the double-edged nature of industrial revolution: While rapid creation of modern technology helped people stay interconnected and made life generally easier, it also replaced physical interaction with virtual, and the need to learn with ignorance. Vashti doesn't need to travel to be with her son or friends. In fact, she prefers to stay in her room because "she could scarcely spare the time for a visit," (Forster, p. 2) sacrificing the opportunity for real connection. Vashti also doesn't care to learn how the technology that surrounds her functions, because she believes "the Machine is omnipotent, eternal; blessed." (Forster, p. 19) Forster extends this idea of technological ignorance to an extreme scenario, where nobody knows how the Machine works anymore because technology and religion have become one. When the Machine, the single supporting structure of comfortable living catastrophically fails, there is no coming back. Repair is not an option because god-like technology eliminates the need for knowledge.

Transportation for the Physically and Psychologically Weak

Transportation in "The Machine Stops" is the best representation of the deterioration of human strength initiated by the development of and reliance on supposedly helpful technology. The self-propelled chairs and floor inside the Machine are symbolic of physical weakness. They are built for the indolent, just like the rest of life in the Machine. They remove the need for walking, reaching, and bending down. When a book fell on the gangway outside the Machine, the book's owner watched in disbelief as the "sacred volume lay motionless." (Forster, p. 7) Although not commonplace today, the mechanical chairs demonstrate how technologies that physically accommodate every desire and necessity will produce feeble and helpless people who cannot stand up for themselves.

Similarly, the air-ship scene, which echoes the Wright brothers' first powered flight in 1903, is the story's representation of psychological weakness. Having stayed in a hexagonal cell for most of her life, Vashti has an irrational repulsion of physical contact and the presence of strangers. (Forster, p. 9) Air travel, which in Forster's time had appeared so remarkable and optimistic, now stood as merely an afterthought in Vashti's overwhelming abhorrence of public transportation. The story uses the air-ship and Vashti's odd behavior to reveal the ever-rising standards set by technological innovation, and how something so advanced can quickly become unbearable simply because people constantly expect more. As dependence on technology grows, Forster believes that people will become unfulfilled, easily upset, and physically, psychologically, and emotionally fragile beings.

Obsolescence of Knowledge and Refusal of Learning

Now weakened and inept, technological overdependence deals its final blow by eliminating the need for knowledge entirely. The most harmful outcome of life in the Machine is the erasure of fact and the replacement of truth. The lecturers in the story spread ideas second-hand. They preach to their audiences to "beware of first-hand ideas," (Forster, p. 18) because facts fall second to what people want to hear and say. Learning and education in "The Machine Stops" are superficial, performative concepts that are used solely to occupy space in the day. Lectures contain no useful information, and history serves no purpose because culture, knowledge, and experience has become homogenized and fully regulated by technology that nobody understands. Furthermore, knowledge itself has become obsolete, as the Book of the Machine has become accepted as the highest truth. Forster writes about the Book as a religious text. Vashti even prays with it. (Forster, p. 24) Like many religious texts, the Book also functions as an object of comfort. The information within it is secondary to its symbolic representation of people's unwavering faith to and dependency on the Machine. When people are truly convinced that their faith provides them all the information they need, all other knowledge becomes unimportant. Technology becomes god, and people become incapable of critical thinking and blind to harm.

One Hundred Years Later

Written over a century ago, Forster's warning holds frighteningly pertinent for contemporary technological developments. The recent popularization of large language models and advancement of highly specialized, publicly-accessible artificial intelligence has had huge impacts on human behavior, lowering the effort required to complete various creative and intellectual tasks by offloading mental energy, learning, and thinking to a machine. The threat from this level of technological dependence cannot be understated. As a society, we are actively walking in the Machine's footsteps. Today's technology has crossed that line between technological progress and self destruction. With each new generation of AI, we trade more agency for agents and thinking for tokens. As machines acquire more skills, our own will inevitably atrophy. Be very careful with how you decide to involve yourself with these new technologies. Wonder how they will affect you, your learning, your creativity, your thought. Question how they will affect the world, the media, education, the distribution of power and wealth. Make sure you can carry on when the machine stops.
